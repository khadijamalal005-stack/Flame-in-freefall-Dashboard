Evidence from PSI: does the 1 g model hold in microgravity?

Case study built on PSI-25 (BASS-II, DOI 10.60555/4qc4-de67) and PSI-26 (BASS, DOI 10.60555/58h4-dy80), both CC0-1.0, and on NASA’s ISS Researcher’s Guide to Combustion Science (ISS Program Science Office, NP-2015-10-034-JSC, 2015, which predates some later experiments). Only statements traceable to the PSI page, the guide or the cited publications are listed. Nothing here is a number computed by our team.

What BASS-II tested

Solid fuels (PMMA rods, sheets and slabs, and cotton-fiberglass fabric) burned in the Microgravity Science Glovebox on the ISS. Variables: airflow speed (up to 40 cm/s), oxygen concentration (lowered below 21% with nitrogen) and sample geometry. Its hypothesis: with adequate ventilation, a material burns as well or better in microgravity than in normal gravity.

Oxygen range explored

Olson and Ferkul (2017, Combustion and Flame, DOI 10.1016/j.combustflame.2017.03.001) report the first concurrent-flow rod flammability tests in microgravity: flows from 0 to 55 cm/s, clear and black PMMA rods, oxygen varied from 13.6% to 22.2%.

Lower limits than on Earth

Olson et al. (49th ICES, 2019, “Flammability Limits from BASS-II Testing in Microgravity Compared to Normal Gravity Limits”) report flame extinctions indicating that materials can burn at a lower oxygen concentration in microgravity than in normal gravity.

Material grade matters

A BASS presentation (Ferkul et al., 2nd Annual ISS Research and Development Conference, Denver, July 2013) reports that Ultem and Nomex HT90-40 (upward-burning limit 23.5% O₂) did not burn in ISS air (21–22% O₂, about 15 cm/s), while Nomex III (limit 22% O₂) did, at 22% O₂ and 14 cm/s.

Airflow sets the limit

NASA’s guide (pp. 18, 31) describes a U-shaped flammability boundary: in low-flow conditions some materials burn at lower oxygen than on Earth. The minimum sits near 10 cm/s, the typical speed of spacecraft ventilation, which is also where flame spread is fastest.

A 1 g test may not be conservative

The guide (p. 31) reports that recent BASS results indicate NASA-STD-6001 Test 1 is not conservative. It adds (p. 18) that the assumed safety margin from quiescent tests may not hold where ventilation strongly enhances flames.

Three misconceptions

The guide (p. 35) lists beliefs that shaped spacecraft fire safety: that a steady fire cannot exist in quiescent microgravity, that a fire goes out if ventilation stops, and that terrestrial fire standards are conservative. Research showed all three to be false.

Suppression and detection differ

Nitrogen applied locally can strengthen a flame by entraining oxygen (p. 22). Smoke particles differ in microgravity, so Earth detectors may look for the wrong signature (p. 35). Water-mist extinguishers began replacing CO₂ units on the ISS in 2015 (p. 26).

How microgravity is reached

According to NASA Glenn’s outreach page: the 2.2 Second Drop Tower gives 2.2 s of microgravity (a 24 m fall), the Zero Gravity Research Facility about 5 s (132 m, operating since 1966) and the KC-135 aircraft about 15 s per parabola. Only the ISS allows long tests, which is why PSI investigations such as BASS-II matter.

What BASS (PSI-26) burned

PSI lists 123 test rows, the ones we read dating from March 2012 to April 2013. Fuels: a 75% cotton / 25% fiberglass fabric (SIBAL), Nomex, Ultem, PMMA spheres, sheets and slabs, and wax candles, in a 76 mm × 76 mm flow duct with airflow up to 40 cm/s, in opposed or concurrent flow. A nitrogen suppressant was also assessed.

Why Test 1 matters (PSI-26)

PSI-26 states that NASA spacecraft materials selection rests on NASA-STD-6001 Test 1, which ranks materials by 1 g behavior without considering low-gravity effects, and that the flame’s radiative emission is a critical element. A related paper (Takahashi et al., DOI 10.1016/j.proci.2014.07.017) proposes a simplified model for the difference between thin-material flammability limits in normal gravity and microgravity.

BASS overview results (2013)

By July 2013, 41 samples and 115 burns had been completed. For thin fuels, opposed-flow spread was fastest at an intermediate flow speed, while concurrent-flow spread rose linearly with flow and quenched near 1 cm/s; at about 10 cm/s the slide gives 2.55 mm/s versus 1.21 mm/s for concurrent versus opposed flow. PMMA spheres and paraffin candles kept burning at 17% O₂ in very slow flow. A nitrogen jet of 500 cc/min weakened but did not extinguish a wake-stabilized PMMA flame.

A real fire in orbit: Saffire-I

NASA reports that Saffire-I set a large-scale fire intentionally inside a spacecraft in orbit for the first time. It burned a 94 × 40.6 cm cotton–fiberglass fabric in concurrent and opposed flow at an average 20 cm/s. In concurrent flow the flame stayed size-limited after ignition, unlike an upward flame on Earth, and it spread more slowly in the larger tunnel than in earlier small ones, which shows why full-scale tests matter (NASA NTRS 20170008805).

Pressure changes flammability

Thomsen et al. (ICES 2018) compared Saffire microgravity spread rates (100 kPa, 20 cm/s) with upward spread at reduced pressure on the same fabric: similar rates appeared around 30 kPa, and lower pressure weakened the flame. A NASA Glenn presentation (NTRS 20150000894) reports a flammability boundary close to linear in oxygen partial pressure and notes that a fire-resistant material can become flammable depending on ambient pressure. Our Lab’s pressure exponent (0.8) is a heuristic, not fitted to these data.

What this means for our Flammability Lab: its index starts from limiting oxygen index values measured at 1 g, so it may underestimate risk in microgravity for some materials, and a generic material name hides differences between grades, and it does not model airflow even though the guide shows airflow sets the limit. We have not yet recalibrated it. Next step: quantify with the BASS-II experimental table and the BASS-II Summary Report listed on the PSI-25 page.

Investigation summaries in plain language

Earth Applications

The challenge asks how PSI combustion research can improve fire safety on Earth. Examples named in the challenge statement and its resource list (NASA Spinoff and NASA articles):

Fine water mist extinguishers

Developed for use on the ISS, with practical applications on Earth. Non-toxic water-mist portable extinguishers were certified for spaceflight, and the first two of nine planned units reached the ISS in December 2015 to replace CO₂ units (NASA Researcher’s Guide, p. 26). Closest PSI investigations (our mapping): FLEX, FLEX-2.

Smoke detection: the Fire Detector spinoff

NASA Spinoff (1978): the Brunswick Incipient Fire Detector, first used in space shuttle orbiters, samples air for combustion particles and can tell them apart from dust, so it is less prone to false alarms. It signals a fire condition before flame and smoke appear. It was trialled in mining and in a military aircraft. Closest PSI investigations (our mapping): SAME, SAME-R, DAFT, SPICE.

Firefighter gear: NASA spinoffs

Fireman’s Air Tanks (NASA Spinoff, 1976), developed with the Johnson Space Center and Scott Aviation: a 30-minute air supply weighing 20 lb, 13 lb lighter than conventional tanks, pressurized at 4,500 psi, with an aluminum liner wrapped in resin-impregnated glass fibers that removes corrosion. A 1980s spinoff also adapted NASA spacesuit technology to firefighter clothing. Closest PSI investigations (our mapping): BASS, BASS-II, SAFFIRE.

Lessons from a real fire: Mir, 1997

On 24 February 1997 an oxygen-generator canister ignited aboard Mir, throwing a three-foot flame with sparks and molten metal, filling the station with smoke and blocking one of two Soyuz escape vehicles. The crew used masks and foam extinguishers, with no lasting harm. Investigators concluded that hydrocarbons in the canister, most likely latex glove material, raised the risk. The lessons shaped ISS design (NASA, 2022).

Cleaner, more efficient burning

In NASA’s ScienceCasts video (2018), ACME project scientist Dennis Stocker says about 85% of the energy we use comes from combustion and about 70% of home and workplace electricity from combustion power plants. Without gravity, flames do not flicker, which allows more precise measurements and could lead to burning fuel more efficiently with less soot and pollution. ACME began in November 2017 with six major experiments of about five months each. Closest PSI investigations (our mapping): the six ACME entries (BRE, CLD Flame, E-FIELD, Flame Design, s-Flame, CFI-G).
